Videos

  • Unit 1: In our ‘Economist in action’ video, Thomas Piketty and James Heckman discuss how tax records and other data can be used to provide a social context for our understanding of economics. They argue that this context can create a more detailed narrative for understanding today’s economic challenges.
  • Unit 1: Professor Bishnupriya Gupta explains the long period of economic stagnation, and even decline, during British rule in India and considers why economic prosperity accelerated following Indian independence in 1947.
  • Unit 2: In our ‘Economist in action’ video, economic historian Bob Allen addresses the question of why the Industrial Revolution happened in Britain before other countries. He shows how the rise in women’s earnings prompted mechanisation in spinning.
  • Unit 2: In our ‘Economist in action’ video, economic historian Suresh Naidu explains how population growth, technological development, and political events interacted to produce the real wage hockey stick.
  • Unit 3: In our ‘Economist in action’ video, Juliet Schor charts the historic fall and recent rise of working hours in the USA and the UK. Juliet discusses how technological advances increase productivity and how this affects workers’ levels of consumption, free time, and a country’s environmental impact.
  • Unit 4: The game theory scenario of the prisoners’ dilemma is turned on its head in this clip from the game show ‘Golden Balls’. One contestant manages to trick the other into a mutually beneficial arrangement, making for an unusual twist on a classic economics problem.
  • Unit 4: In our ‘Economist in action’ video, Juan Camilo Cárdenas describes how his experiments help explain why people cooperate even when there are incentives not to. Juan Camilo discusses how inequality and social context affect cooperation, and what these can tell us about tackling the biggest cooperation problem: climate change.
  • Unit 6: Economist Anna Vitali and her collaborators have studied how people find jobs. They look at the matching process in labour markets in 15 cities in Uganda, a country where the flow of information between job seekers and firms is poor. They found that enabling job seekers to share their information with employers did not have the intended effect of improving the matching process, highlighting the problem that workers can get discouraged and withdraw into inactivity.
  • Unit 6: In our ‘Economist in action’ video, Arin Dube describes his study on the effect of minimum wage changes in different American states. On average, Arin found that raising the minimum wage increased the income of poor workers, did not reduce employment and reduced the Gini coefficient.
  • Unit 8: In our ‘Economist in action’ video, Kathryn Graddy explains her study of the Fulton fish market. Kathryn observed that sellers were pricing their products differently depending on the race of their buyer, and explains what this teaches us about perfect competition.
  • Unit 9: This New York Times video looks at the use of ignition disabling devices in the United States. These devices prevent credit recipients from driving if they fail to make their loan repayments - the video examines the impact this has on loan recipients.
  • Unit 10: In our ‘Economist in action’ video, Nobel laureate Alvin Roth looks at the concept of matching markets through the lens of kidney transplants and indentured servitude. Alvin explains how matching markets facilitate mutually beneficial trades in cases where market economies are not appropriate.
  • Unit 10: In his TED Talk ‘Why we shouldn’t trust markets with our civic life’, Michael Sandel explains the transition from a market economy to a market society. Sandel examines what this means for inequality and shares his concern that not everything should be up for sale.
